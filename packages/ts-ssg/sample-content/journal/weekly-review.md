# A weekly review people will actually read

A useful project update lets someone understand the state of the work without arranging another meeting. It answers four questions: what moved, what is next, what needs a decision, and where the detail lives.

## Start with the outcome

Lead with one sentence that describes the change in the project, not the amount of activity around it.

> The onboarding flow is ready for final review; the launch date still depends on accessibility sign-off.

That sentence gives readers a current picture. The details underneath can support it.

## Use a small, repeatable structure

| Section | What belongs here |
| --- | --- |
| Progress | Milestones completed or moved forward |
| Next | The two or three actions that matter most |
| Decisions | A question, an owner, and a target date |
| Risks | A blocker and the help needed to remove it |

Keep each section short. Link to a [workspace view](/workspace/) for the full trail of tasks and decisions.

## Write for the next reader

A future teammate may open the update without having attended the meeting. Name the owner, spell out the decision, and avoid shorthand that only the current team understands.

For example, “waiting on review” is hard to act on. “Maya will approve the final email copy by Thursday” tells everyone what happens next.

## End with a clear ask

If the team needs help, say exactly what would unblock the work. If nothing is blocked, say that too. A calm, explicit update builds more trust than a long list of completed tasks.

The Waypoint starter uses this article to demonstrate long-form Markdown, a table of contents, a quotation, a table, and internal links. The writing pattern also works in a real team.
